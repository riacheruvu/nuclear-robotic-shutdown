View the controller math and why the bands reset

Each robot runs the actual proportional control law from the paper: \(v_{cmd} = -K_p \cdot (\text{observed cross-track error})\), with \(K_p = 2.5\), passed through a 2-step FIFO lag buffer before it takes effect. That is the same delay structure used in the paper's 7D state. The controller reacts to the corrupted observation, not the true position, which is the whole reason noise matters here. The two dashed bands are a real axis-aligned interval reachability propagation, \(\Delta s_{t+1} \approx |A|\Delta s_t + |B|W\), on the reduced [cross-track, lag] state. The amber band is certified against the terrestrial bound, and the violet one against the non-terrestrial bound.

Why the bands reset every 22 steps: a naive axis-aligned interval box propagated through this feedback loop without resetting explodes within a few dozen steps. That is not because the real robot is unsafe, but because interval arithmetic cannot represent the cancellation between cross-track error and the lagged correction that is actually steering it back. This is the same "interval blow-up" the paper names as a limitation of full nonlinear reachability over long horizons. Real qualification frameworks handle this with tighter re-linearization, zonotopes, or ellipsoidal sets instead of plain boxes. The rolling reset here stands in for that fix honestly, rather than pretending to be the real thing.

Show how each slider maps to the math (with a worked example)
A
Source activity, slider "A", symbol \(A\), unit Ci (Curies)

This feeds directly into \(\dot D(r)=\frac{\Gamma A}{r^2}\). Because \(A\) sits on top of the fraction, it scales the dose rate proportionally. If you double \(A\), you double the dose rate. This simply represents how strong the radioactive source is.

r
Distance to source, slider "r", symbol \(r\), unit meters

This feeds into the denominator, squared: \(\dot D(r)=\frac{\Gamma A}{r^2}\). This is the dial that matters most. Halve the distance and the dose rate goes up 4x, not 2x, because \(r\) is squared. Moving from 2m to 1m quadruples the dose, and moving from 1m to 0.5m quadruples it again. That single inverse-square relationship is why the last meter of approach to the valve is always the most dangerous.

psp
P(scramble), slider "Terrestrial scramble chance", symbol \(p_{sp}\), unitless probability

This is the coin-flip weight in the salt-and-pepper equation. It acts as a Bernoulli gate that triggers a scramble with probability \(p_{sp}\) on any given step, independent of distance or dose. Over a 100-step mission, the expected number of scramble events is roughly \(p_{sp}\) times 100. At the default setting of 0.05, that equals about 5 scrambles per mission on average.

pseu
P(bit-flip), slider "Deep-space bit-flip chance", symbol \(p_{seu}\), unitless probability

This plays the same role as \(p_{sp}\), but it gates the SEU equation instead. The chance of an event happening is the same kind of coin flip. What is different is what happens after the coin flip. A salt-and-pepper hit is always roughly the same size, while a bit-flip hit's size depends entirely on which bit got hit.

·
Worked example using the default numbers

\(A = 15\) Ci, \(r = 1\) m: \(\dot D = \frac{\Gamma A}{r^2} = \frac{1.32 \times 15}{1^2} = \mathbf{19.8}\) R/hr.
Convert to mSv/hr: \(19.8 \times 8.77 = \mathbf{173.6}\) mSv/hr.
Convert to per-simulation-step (each step is about 1 second here): \(173.6 \div 3600 \approx \mathbf{0.048}\) mSv/step.
That is the exact number the gauge above is showing you in real time as you move the \(A\) and \(r\) sliders. It is the same arithmetic, recalculated every 90 milliseconds.

fixed
Constants you cannot move on purpose, and what they mean

\(\sigma_g = 0.01\) m: the Gaussian noise floor's width. This is not a slider because it represents a fixed sensor specification rather than something that varies from mission to mission.
\(R_{\max} = 0.6\) m: how far a salt-and-pepper scramble can throw the reading, capped by definition.
\(N = 8\) bits: how many bits represent the position internally. This sets how big the worst-case SEU jump can be.
\(\Gamma = 1.32\) R·m²/(hr·Ci): a physical constant for Co-60 specifically, not something you would typically tune.

The core concepts

plain language explanations
Dose rate

This is a measure of how intense the radiation is exactly where the robot is standing right now. It gets much worse the closer you get to the source. Cutting the distance in half means the intensity becomes four times stronger, rather than just doubling.

Gaussian drift

Think of this as a faint, constant wobble in the position reading. Every sensor has this, even with zero radiation present. It is like the static on an old radio: always there, but never huge.

Salt-and-pepper scramble

This is a sudden, random glitch where the camera or LiDAR reading is briefly corrupted. It looks like static flashing across a TV screen for a single frame. It either happens or it doesn't, and when it does, the incorrect reading is usually bounded to a fixed radius around the true position.

Bit-flip (SEU)

A high-energy particle flips a single 0 to a 1 (or back) somewhere inside the computer's memory. Since numbers are stored as bits, flipping the wrong one can cause the position estimate to jump drastically across the environment rather than just nudging it slightly.

Why this matters: salt-and-pepper events are gated by a coin flip, but the size of the hit is bounded. A strike either scrambles the reading or it doesn't, and when it does, it stays within a fixed radius. SEU bit-flips are gated by the same kind of coin flip, but the size depends on which bit flips. A low-order bit barely moves the estimate, while flipping the most significant bit causes a jump scaled to the full representable range. They share the same trigger, but have very different consequences.

Assumptions & limitations

for technical reviewers, click to expand
Review the 9 assumptions behind this model
01
Independence across steps

Each step's noise draw is generated independently. Real radiation events from a single particle shower or a sustained source excursion can cluster in time. This model has no mechanism for burst-correlated strikes.

Challenge: would a Markov-modulated Bernoulli process (bursty on/off states) change the tail shape more than the per-step probability does?
02
\(R_{\max}\) and bit-width \(N\) are illustrative constants

Scramble radius (0.6 m) and bit-width (8) are round numbers chosen for visual clarity. They are not derived from a specific sensor datasheet or a specific embedded representation in deployed hardware.

Challenge: what is the actual fixed-point or floating-point width used in your POMDP's belief-state representation, and does that change which bit dominates the tail?
03
The SEU model is an analogy, not a validated parameterization

It extends the "MSB-flip causes detection collapse" argument from quantized neural-weight literature to a positional state. No source in the paper's bibliography models position-encoding bit-flips directly, so this is a reasonable extension rather than a citation.

Challenge: is there empirical SEU data on position/pose registers specifically (vs. weight memory or framebuffers) that would replace this analogy with a measured distribution?
04
Gaussian \(\sigma_g\) is a placeholder, not a sensor spec

\(\sigma_g = 0.01\) m stands in for the generic encoder and thermal noise floor. It isn't pulled from a specific LIDAR or visual-odometry datasheet.

Challenge: swap in your actual sensor's noise spec. Does Gaussian drift stay negligible relative to the discrete events, or does it start to matter at longer horizons?
05
R-to-mSv conversion factor is approximate

8.77 mSv per roentgen is a standard air-kerma-to-tissue approximation. The real conversion depends on the photon energy spectrum (Co-60 specifically) and exposure geometry, which this model doesn't separately track.

Challenge: for a 15 Ci Co-60 source specifically, does the energy-dependent conversion shift the cumulative-dose numbers enough to matter for a 50 mSv threshold?
06
The controller is real; the reachability state is reduced-order

The trajectory panel now runs the actual P-controller (\(K_p = 2.5\)) with the actual 2-step FIFO lag buffer from the paper, reacting to the corrupted observation exactly as described. One thing that is still simplified is the interval reachability. It propagates a 3-state channel [cross-track error, lag1, lag2] instead of the full 7D state, and it resets every 22 steps to stay legible instead of running open-loop for the full horizon.

Challenge: does propagating the full 7D Jacobian (rather than this collapsed cross-track channel) actually delay the blow-up, or does heading coupling make it worse?
07
Naive interval arithmetic over-inflates feedback loops

Run the 3-state propagation above open-loop (no reset) and it diverges within roughly 50 to 60 steps, even though the real controller is provably stable. That is not a bug. Axis-aligned interval (box) arithmetic cannot represent the correlation between cross-track error and the lagged correction term that is actually cancelling it out in reality. Because of this, the bound inflates faster than any real trajectory could. This is the exact same "interval blow-up" the paper flags as a reason full nonlinear reachability over long horizons needs careful handling.

Challenge: would a zonotope or ellipsoidal reachable set (which preserve cross-dimension correlation) stay tight over the full 100 steps without needing a rolling reset?
08
Noise sources are shown in isolation, not superimposed

Each card and each trajectory in this artifact is driven by exactly one noise source at a time for clarity. A real deployed system faces continuous Gaussian drift and discrete scrambles simultaneously, and the combination is not strictly additive once a controller is in the loop.

Challenge: does running all three noise sources on one robot simultaneously change the failure rate non-linearly, the way it did when your IS estimator initially missed the Bernoulli scrambles entirely?
09
Idealized point source, no shielding or scattering

The dose-rate law assumes a bare point source in free space. Real facility geometry, like walls, scattering, and partial shielding, will change the actual field shape away from a clean inverse-square falloff.

Challenge: how much does a realistic containment geometry flatten or distort the gradient relative to this idealization, and does that change where the "danger zone" actually starts?
